# Faliscan with a Latin model: where we are and the plan to 15 December

**Date: 2026-11-25.** Draft for collaborators. Please comment on anything that looks
wrong, especially section 3 (the change of method) and section 7 (what we need from you).

## 1. What we are doing

Aeneas is a neural model trained on about 180,000 Latin inscriptions. Given a damaged
text it restores missing letters, estimates a date and a region, and retrieves similar
inscriptions ("parallels"). **We want to make these tools usable on Faliscan, without
training a new model.**

- **Why it might work.** Faliscan is very close to Latin. Much of the difference is in
  spelling and in a limited set of sound correspondences (*foied* = *hodie*,
  *carefo* = *carebo*, *-o* = *-us* / *-um*).
- **Why it does not work out of the box.** The model has never seen Faliscan and reads
  its spelling as noise. In our first baseline run, hiding one surviving letter and
  asking the model to restore it, it was right 83% of the time on Latin texts and 42%
  on Faliscan texts of the same lengths.
- **Why we cannot simply retrain.** The whole Faliscan corpus is about 10,000
  characters. That is far too little to train on.

**The approach:** turn the Faliscan text into Latin spelling automatically, using the
linguists' correspondence table together with the model's own knowledge of Latin, and
then run the unchanged model on the result.

**What the contribution is, and is not.**

- The Latin-spelled text is **not** the contribution. The rewriting is done for the
  model's benefit: the model cannot use its Latin knowledge on `hec cupat`, and it can
  on *hic cubat*. For a Faliscan specialist the rewriting is redundant (they read
  `cauio` as *Gavius* at once). For a Latin reader who is not a specialist it is a
  convenience: with the correspondence table and some effort they could work out most
  forms themselves.
- What the model offers that a reader does not already have: **parallels** from
  180,000 Latin inscriptions, **ranked proposals for damaged places** with
  probabilities, and independent **date and region** estimates. At present none of
  these work properly on Faliscan.
- **Why automate the rewriting** when a specialist could do it by hand? For Faliscan
  alone the case is modest (464 texts), and the paper will say so. But the automatic
  version keeps uncertain places open with probabilities instead of committing to one
  reading; the same recipe applies to other poorly attested varieties close to a
  well-attested language; and the hand-made version serves as our upper bound.
- **A measurement as a by-product:** how much of the model's difficulty with Faliscan
  disappears when only the spelling is regularised, and what remains (so far: about
  three quarters of the differences in the test texts are spelling, a quarter
  morphology and vocabulary).

**Intended users:** people who read Latin, whether or not they are Faliscan
specialists (epigraphers and historians, students, museum and database staff,
interested amateurs with Latin). Everything the model returns is Latin, so it is of
little use to someone with no Latin. What these users get is mainly the parallels and
the ranked proposals for damaged places; for non-specialists, the per-place
explanations (where the text departs from Latin spelling, what each place probably
stands for, how sure the model is) are a secondary aid. The model's confidence has to
be trustworthy, first because wrong rewrites mislead the model in everything that
follows, and second because a reader may rely on them; testing it is a central result.
We have no user study; this is the intended use, not something we will have shown.

**Target:** an application paper for an EACL workshop, deadline **15 December 2026**.
We aim for a minimal complete version by then, with results that can be scored
automatically. Expert judgement of the parallels is not required for this version.

**The last item in brief.** We respelled one place in Latin texts the Faliscan way
(*quintus* → *cuintus*, *sacrum* → *sacro*, *bene* → *pene*) and looked inside the
model:

- It already treats *cu* as close to *qu*, and *-o* as a plausible ending.
- It does not treat *p* as close to *b* any more than other letters.
- There is no "Faliscan direction" inside the model. Most of the effect of a Faliscan
  letter is the effect of any replaced letter.

### How the model finds parallels, and what it returns for Faliscan now

**How retrieval works** (read from the code and checked):

1. The text goes through the model, one position per character.
2. The output of the final layer is **averaged over all positions** of the text. This
   average (384 numbers) is the text's embedding.
3. The embeddings of all 180,110 inscriptions in the corpus are stored in advance. The
   query is compared with each by cosine similarity, after standardising each
   dimension, and the most similar inscriptions are returned.

There is no separate retrieval model: the embedding is a by-product of training on
restoration, dating and region. We recomputed the embedding for 11 corpus texts and
all match the stored ones exactly, so the stored file was made with this checkpoint
and this averaging (`colabs/faliscan_baseline/checks/check_retrieval_emb.py`).

Because the embedding is an average over characters, spelling enters it directly.

**What the unchanged model returns for raw Faliscan** (baseline, top parallels):

| Faliscan text | Top parallels returned | What they appear to be |
|---|---|---|
| EF 3 `ecoquto euotenosio titias duenom...` | `Licnos Contextos iereu...`, `Bratronos Nantonti...` | Gaulish inscriptions |
| MLF 313 `uolta uelmineo fuloniacue` | `ACOS Olsailacos Belsadin...` | from Spain, not Latin |
| LF 221 `uipia zertenea loferta...` | `Brata Polf Sa anacta Ceri` (Samnium, 200 BCE) | a Sabellic text |
| MF 11-12 `uolti teti cauio...` | `Menio Fasia Acila Ceisia Loucilia...` (Latium, 330 BCE); `mamar ce vel ... turuce` (Etruria, 600 BCE) | early Latin names; Etruscan |
| MF 146 `cauio leuelio caui hileo...` | `Eco urna Tita Vendias Mamar...` (Etruria, 630 BCE) | an archaic inscription from the region |

- **The model files Faliscan under "odd text".** Its nearest neighbours are whatever
  else in the corpus does not look like normal Latin: Gaulish, Etruscan, Sabellic,
  fragmentary texts. (The language labels above are our reading of the texts; the
  corpus does not record them. Corrections welcome.)
- **A few are relevant, most are not.** The archaic texts from Etruria and Latium are
  the kind of thing one might cite; a Gaulish potter's stamp is not a parallel to a
  Faliscan tomb inscription.
- **Regions are scattered:** among the top 3 parallels of the 35 texts the commonest
  are Latium et Campania (13), Aquitania (11), Hispania citerior (10), Lugdunensis (9).
- **A smaller effect of the endings:** 34% of words in the Faliscan texts end in *-o*,
  against 18% in their parallels and 11% in the corpus.

So rewriting has a concrete job on parallels: move the text out of the "odd text"
neighbourhood, so that it retrieves Latin inscriptions of the same kind (tomb formulae,
dedications, names). The caution is that some of the "odd" neighbours are the archaic
regional texts that may be the best parallels, and full normalisation could lose them.

## 3. A change of method

*Status: under discussion between us; to be revisited. The reasoning below is the
current view, not a settled decision.*

**The earlier plan** had two methods: (1) rewrite Faliscan into Latin spelling with
rules; (2) find the effect of each Faliscan spelling inside the model and cancel it
there ("steering").

**Why we are dropping (2) as the main method:**

- To the model, a Faliscan spelling is just a different letter. Cancelling its effect
  inside the model amounts to replacing the letter, which is what (1) already does.
- Both methods need to know *where* to act, and that is the hard part. From Latin to
  Faliscan the rules are mechanical (every *qu* becomes *cu*). From Faliscan to Latin
  they are not: a *p* is sometimes Latin *b* and sometimes *p*; a final *-o* can be
  *-us*, *-um* or a real *-o*. Rules alone cannot decide, and steering does not help.

What we learned from the work on (2) stays in the paper as analysis (section 2, last
item): it shows which Faliscan spellings the model already tolerates.

## 4. The new method: the rules propose, the model chooses

Example: `foied uino pipafo cra carefo` (Latin *hodie vinum bibam cras carebo*).

**Step 1. The rules list the options at each place where they could apply,** always
including "leave it as it is".

| Word | Place | Options |
|---|---|---|
| `uino` | final *-o* | *-o*, *-us*, *-um* |
| `pipafo` | each *p* | *p*, *b* |
| `pipafo`, `carefo` | *f* inside the word | *f*, *b*, *d* |
| `foied` | initial *f* | *f*, *h* |

**Step 2. The model scores the options.** We hide the place, and the model's
restoration output gives a probability for each letter in that context. We look only
at the options the rule allows. The model is asked "which of these Latin spellings fits
here", which is what it was trained to do.

**Step 3. Most confident first, then repeat.** The place where the model is most
certain is fixed first. The others are scored again, because the context now looks
more like Latin. This continues until every place is decided, or until the model is
too uncertain; uncertain places are left unchanged or reported as undecided.

The result is a Latin-spelled version of the Faliscan text, with a confidence for each
change. The unchanged model is then run on it for restoration, dating, region and
parallels.

**Three versions, each giving more of the work to the model:**

| Version | Where to change | What to change it to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | the rules | rules propose, model chooses |
| 2 | the model flags letters it finds improbable | rules propose, model chooses |
| 3 | the model | the model, unrestricted |

Version 1 is the core. Version 3 needs no rules, and so no linguist, but is likely to
rewrite rare correct words into common Latin ones. It has two uses:

- **It shows how much the table contributes,** by comparison with versions 1 and 2.
- **Can the Latin model rediscover the correspondences?** Run version 3 over the whole
  Faliscan corpus and collect the changes it makes consistently (for example *p* to
  *b*, *c* to *g*, final *-o* to *-us*). This gives a correspondence table produced by
  the model, which can be compared with YQ's table and with the changes we found
  missing from it (appendix A.2). If it recovers known correspondences, that is direct
  evidence that a Latin model can do useful work on Faliscan without being told
  anything about it; consistent changes that are not in the table are candidates for
  YQ to look at; and if it finds little, the table is doing the work. This is also the
  version that matters where no table exists, such as another poorly attested variety
  close to Latin. No expert scoring is needed, since YQ's table is the reference.

### Which rules need the model at all

Many correspondences are one-to-one from Latin to Faliscan (every Latin *b* is written
*p*) but not from Faliscan to Latin (a Faliscan *p* is Latin *b* or *p*). How often
each change actually applies, in the 35 test texts and, for comparison, in ordinary
Latin:

| Faliscan spelling | Places in the 35 texts | Need changing | Share in Latin text that would be the changed form |
|---|---|---|---|
| *cu* before a vowel → *qu* | 5 | 5 (100%) | 97% |
| *z* → *s* | 6-7 | all | Latin has almost no *z* |
| *e* before a vowel → *i* | 24 | 19 (79%) | 88% |
| final *-o* → *-us* / *-um* | 57 | 47 (82%) | 62% (*-us* 49%, *-um* 13%, real *-o* 38%) |
| *p* → *b* | 48 | 16 (33%) | 34% |
| *c*, *k*, *q* → *g* | 94 | 19 (20%) | 23% |
| *e* elsewhere → *i* or *ae* | about 112 | about 27 (24%) | *ae* is 16% of *e* + *ae* |
| add a final consonant (*s, m, c, r, t*) | 146 words ending in a vowel | 19 (13%) | not counted |

(Counts for the 35 texts are approximate: places are counted in words without gaps,
and the changes come from our hand-made Latin equivalents, not yet confirmed by YQ.)

So the rows fall into three groups, marked in appendix A:

- **Always:** *cu* → *qu*, *z* → *s*, *k* → *c*, *-os*/*-om* → *-us*/*-um*. Applied
  directly; no choice is needed.
- **Usually:** *e* before a vowel, final *-o*. Applying them blindly is right about
  four times in five; for *-o* there is also the choice between *-us* and *-um*.
- **Sometimes:** *p*, *c*, *e* elsewhere, added final consonants. Applied blindly
  these are wrong two times in three or more (every *pater* would become *bater*), and
  they are frequent letters. *p* → *b*, *c* → *g* and *e* → *i* together account for
  about 60 of the 172 changed words.

**The method therefore has two stages:** (1) apply the "always" rows directly, which
already makes the context more Latin; (2) let the model choose for the "usually" and
"sometimes" rows, as in the steps below.

### Version 1, step by step

Input: a Faliscan text and the option table (appendix A).

0. **Apply the "always" rows** of the option table directly.
1. **Find the candidate places.** Every place in the text where the left-hand side of
   a remaining row of the option table matches, with the row's position condition (word-final,
   word-initial, before a vowel ...). For `pipafo` this gives both *p*, the *f* and the
   final *-o*. Nothing is decided yet.
2. **Score each candidate place.** Replace the place by the model's "missing letter"
   marker and run the model once. Its restoration output gives a probability for every
   letter at that place. Keep only the probabilities of the row's options, including
   the letter as written, and rescale them to sum to 1. When the options differ in
   length (*-o* against *-um*), use the model's marker for a gap of unknown length.
3. **Confidence of a place** = probability of the best option minus that of the second.
4. **Fix one place.** Take the place with the highest confidence and write its best
   option into the text. If the best option is the letter as written, the place is
   closed without a change.
5. **Repeat from step 2** for the remaining places, since the context has changed.
6. **Stop** when no place is left, or when the highest remaining confidence is below a
   threshold. The remaining places keep their Faliscan spelling and are listed as
   undecided, with their options and probabilities.
7. **Output:** the Latin-spelled text, and for every candidate place the choice and its
   confidence. The unchanged model is then run on this text for restoration, dating,
   region and parallels.

What people supply: the option table, once. What the model does: every decision in
every text. The threshold and the order (most confident first, against left to right,
against all at once) are set on Latin respelled as Faliscan, not on the 35 texts.

### Version 2, step by step

The difference from version 1 is step 1 only: the model, not the table, says where to
look.

1. **Find the surprising places.** Hide each letter of the text in turn and read the
   probability the model gives to the letter that is actually written. Flag the letters
   whose probability is below a threshold. The threshold is set on ordinary Latin, so
   that only a small share of normal Latin letters is flagged.
2. **Get the options for each flagged place.**
   - *Version 2a:* if a row of the option table matches there, use its options;
     otherwise leave the place alone. The table is used only to say what a letter may
     become, not where.
   - *Version 2b:* if no row matches, allow any letter. This is the version that could
     find changes the table does not list.
3. **Then as version 1, steps 2 to 7.**

What version 2 can add: fewer false candidates than version 1 (not every *p* is
examined, only the ones the model finds odd), and in 2b, changes missing from the
table. What it cannot do as described: find places where Faliscan leaves a letter out
(*he* for *hec*, *uxo* for *uxor*), because there is no written letter to be surprised
by; those still need the table's rows. Its main risk is that names and rare words are
also surprising to the model.

**Compared against:**

| Condition | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Raw Faliscan text, model unchanged | the starting point (the 42%) |
| Only the "always" rows applied | what the unambiguous rules give for free |
| All rows applied everywhere, no model (every *p* becomes *b*) | what happens without a choice |
| All rows, the model choosing (version 1) | the method; its gap to the row above is what the model contributes |
| Hand-made Latin equivalents of the 35 texts (from `faliscan_test_rule_check.md`, once reviewed) | the upper bound; its gap to the method is the cost of automating |

## 5. How we will measure it

All automatic; no expert time needed for the minimal version.

### 5.1 Restoration accuracy on the 35 real Faliscan texts (main score)

1. **Hide first.** Hide 1-3 letters that survive on the object, as in the baseline. The
   hidden letters are the answer. Hiding comes before rewriting, so that the rewriting
   never sees the letters it will be asked for.
2. **Rewrite the rest.** Run the method on the text with the gap in it.
3. **Restore.** The unchanged model fills the gap, reading Latin-spelled context.
4. **Convert back and compare.** The model answers in Latin spelling and the truth is
   in Faliscan spelling, so the prediction is converted to Faliscan spelling before
   comparing. This is the easy direction (*b* → *p*, *g* → *c*, *qu* → *cu*): in effect,
   if the hidden letter was a Faliscan *p*, both *p* and *b* count as correct.

Two scores are reported:

- **All hidden letters,** with the conversion back.
- **Only hidden letters away from any rule place,** where Faliscan and Latin spelling
  agree and no conversion is needed. This is the stricter number: it measures only
  whether a more Latin-looking context helps the model.

The same hidden letters are used under every condition of the comparison table in
section 4. Hidden letters at a place where the length changes (a final *-o* whose
Latin form is *-us*) are left out of the first score.

### 5.2 Latin respelled as Faliscan

Thousands of held-out Latin texts, respelled with the rules in the Latin → Faliscan
direction. The original is known exactly, so we can measure:

- how often the method recovers the original spelling, per rule;
- **whether the model's confidence can be trusted:** are the places it calls certain
  right more often than the uncertain ones? (Central for the intended users.)
- whether the order matters: most confident first, against left to right, against all
  at once;
- restoration accuracy and how many of the original text's parallels come back.

The mix of decisions is close to the real texts (*p* → *b* in 34% of places in Latin,
33% in the 35 texts; *c* → *g* 23% and 20%), so this test is a fair stand-in. It carries
the statistical weight, since 35 texts are few.

### 5.3 Other measures on the 35 texts

- agreement of the method's choices with the hand-made Latin equivalents;
- date against Bakkum's date range, and the rank of Etruria among the regions;
- **parallels** before and after, shown as examples and read one by one, not only
  counted: we check both that unrelated non-Latin texts (Gaulish, Etruscan) drop out
  and whether relevant archaic texts from the region are lost. Simple counts that
  need no expert: the share of top parallels from Etruria and neighbouring regions,
  and how far their dates are from Bakkum's date for the text. If there is time and willingness,
  a rating of the top parallels for about 10 texts by a collaborator, reported as
  informal and not as a blind expert evaluation.

### 5.4 Checks on Latin

- **Overcorrection, on ordinary Latin:** run the method on held-out Latin texts from
  the model's corpus, unaltered. Nothing should change, so every change the method
  makes is an error we can count.
- **The 9 local Latin texts,** as a small sanity check and not a result (nine texts
  are too few for a firm number):
  - the unchanged model on them, as a middle point between Faliscan and standard
    Latin: how much of the drop comes from old local spelling in general;
  - the method on them: it should not make them worse. They have old and local
    spellings (`gonlegium`, `coirauit`, `eidus`; `Lat 251` has *-o* endings), so some
    changes are legitimate and each one will be looked at by hand.

## 8. Risks and open questions

- **The method is not tested yet.** It may turn out that the model, at 42% on Faliscan,
  is too unsure of the context to choose well, especially on short texts.
- **Options of different lengths** (*-o* against *-um*) need care when comparing
  probabilities. The model has a marker for a gap of unknown length, which we will try
  first.
- **The model prefers common Latin.** It may choose a frequent word over the right one.
- **Only 35 test texts.** Differences between methods on the real texts will be
  noisy; the respelled-Latin test carries the statistical weight.
- **About a quarter of the differences are not spelling** (morphology, obscure words).
  No version of this method addresses them, and the paper will say so.
- **Normalising may remove good parallels.** Archaic and regional Latin texts sit in
  the same "odd text" neighbourhood as Faliscan. A text rewritten into classical
  spelling may stop retrieving them. It may be worth showing parallels for both the
  raw and the rewritten text.
- **Parallels are the motivation but not the main score.** They are what the model
  offers that a reader lacks, but they cannot be scored automatically on real
  Faliscan. The plan uses restoration as the main score, parallel recovery on
  respelled Latin as the automatic stand-in, and examples on the real texts.
- **The practical gain for Faliscan alone is modest** (section 1). The paper should
  present Faliscan as a case study of the recipe.
